# Pyramid Framework

## The Pyramid Principle (Minto)

The pyramid principle structures communication so the main message — the governing thought — sits at the top. Supporting arguments arranged in rigorous logical order below it. The reader gets the answer first, then the justification.

**Why it works:** The mind naturally groups ideas. Organising arguments into a pyramid enlists the reader's pattern-recognition instead of fighting it.

## The Governing Thought

The governing thought is the single claim the entire piece argues for. It must be:

- **A claim, not a topic** — "Acme should accelerate the AI Accelerator hiring to 5 engineers by Q3" not "Acme's AI headcount"
- **Falsifiable** — a reasonable person could disagree with it
- **The answer to the question the audience is actually asking** — not the question you'd prefer they asked

**Tests:**
- "Could someone read this and say 'I disagree'?" If no, it's a topic or description, not a claim.
- "Does this answer the audience's real question?" If no, the governing thought is wrong.
- "Can all supporting arguments be tied back to this?" If not, the pyramid is fractured.

**Common failures:**
- *Observations masquerading as claims:* "AI is transforming Acme's operations" → not falsifiable
- *Topics:* "The AI Accelerator op model" → no claim at all
- *Compound claims:* "We should hire 5 engineers and restructure the intake process" → two governing thoughts; pick one

## MECE Structure

Supporting arguments must be Mutually Exclusive and Collectively Exhaustive.

**Mutually exclusive test:** Does any argument overlap with another? If evidence moves freely between two arguments without feeling wrong, the boundary is wrong.

**Collectively exhaustive test:** Are there important angles the pyramid doesn't address? A well-informed sceptic reading the governing thought should find every reasonable "why?" or "how?" answered somewhere in the structure.

**Common MECE failures:**
- "Cost efficiency" and "budget savings" as separate arguments → overlapping
- A pyramid arguing for hiring 5 engineers that doesn't address the "where do they report?" question → gap
- Three arguments framed as past/present/future when the real structure is people/process/technology → wrong grouping

## Vertical Relationships

Moving up: each idea synthesises the ideas below it. Answers "so what?"

Moving down: each idea answers a question raised by the idea above. Answers "how?", "why?", or "what's the evidence?"

**Testing vertical logic:**
- Does the upper level genuinely synthesise the lower, or is it just a label?
- "Three reasons we should act" above three reasons is a label, not a synthesis. "The costs of inaction exceed the investment" is a synthesis.
- If you can remove the upper-level idea without losing anything, it isn't synthesising — it's just a header.

## Horizontal Relationships

Ideas at the same level relate through either inductive or deductive reasoning.

**Inductive:** A group of similar facts or observations that together justify the level above.
- Example: "Sales declined 12%. Market share dropped 4%. Churn increased 2 points." → "Our commercial position is weakening."
- Use when presenting a body of evidence.

**Deductive:** A logical chain where each idea follows from the previous.
- Pattern: Situation → Complication → Resolution (or Implication)
- Example: "The market is growing 20% annually. We have the strongest product. We should invest to capture share."
- Use when building a single logical argument.

**When to use which:**
- Inductive: presenting multiple data points, evidence, parallel observations
- Deductive: building a single logical chain, particularly in shorter documents
- Note: deductive is harder to follow across long documents — prefer inductive for complex multi-part arguments

## SCR / SCQA Introduction

The introduction sets up the pyramid. It earns the governing thought — it does not repeat it.

**SCR (Situation-Complication-Resolution):**
- **Situation:** Non-controversial, fact-based description of the current state. The audience nods — this is agreed ground.
- **Complication:** Why the situation requires action. What changed, what's at risk, what's the opportunity. Material enough to warrant a call to action.
- **Resolution:** What should be done. The governing thought restated as an action.

**SCQA (Situation-Complication-Question-Answer):**
- Adds an explicit **Question** between complication and resolution.
- Useful when the audience needs to see the question articulated before the answer lands. Effective when the complication is complex or multi-part.

**Tone variants (choose based on audience and purpose):**

| Lead with | When to use |
|-----------|------------|
| Situation | Default for most exec comms — frame the context, then the problem |
| Resolution | Audience already knows the situation — they want the answer fast (decision briefs) |
| Complication | Persuasive pieces where the problem isn't yet acknowledged by the audience |

## Building Top-Down vs Bottom-Up

**Top-down (hypothesis-driven):** You have a candidate governing thought. Build the pyramid by asking "what evidence supports this?" at each level. Fast when you have conviction about the answer.

**Bottom-up (evidence-driven):** You have a pile of evidence but no clear governing thought. Cluster the evidence into groups, ask "so what?" about each group, then ask "so what?" about the level above until the governing thought crystallises. Slower but necessary when the thinking isn't yet done.

Real problems start bottom-up (during analysis) and are expressed top-down (during communication). The communication should almost always be top-down — the audience sees the answer first, then the justification.
